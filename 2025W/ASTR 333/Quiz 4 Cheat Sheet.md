# Quiz 4 Astrobiology / Space Exploration Cheat Sheet

## Exoplanet Demographics

* **Main Sequence Lifetime vs. Stellar Mass:** Lower mass stars live significantly longer. Lifetime is inversely proportional to mass ($t \propto M^{-2.5}$).
* **Luminosity vs. Stellar Mass:** Luminosity increases dramatically with mass ($L \propto M^{3.5}$).
* **Habitable Zone (HZ) vs. Stellar Mass:** The HZ scales with the square root of luminosity ($\sqrt{L}$). Lower mass stars have much closer, tighter HZs.
* **Spectral Classification (Hot to Cool):** O, B, A, F, G, K, M *(Mnemonic: Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me)*.
* **Relative Abundance:** M-dwarfs are the most common (~70-75% of stars in the galaxy). G-type stars (like the Sun) make up about 7%.
* **Notable Systems:**
    * **Barnard's Star:** Closest single star, M-dwarf. Planetary claims have been heavily disputed, but recent data suggests a possible super-Earth.
    * **Trappist-1:** M-dwarf with 7 roughly Earth-sized planets, 3-4 in the HZ, locked in resonant orbits.
    * **Alpha Centauri:** Closest star system (triple star: G, K, and M). The M-dwarf (Proxima Centauri) hosts an Earth-mass planet in the HZ (Proxima b).
* **M-Stars for Astrobiology:**
    * *Pros:* Highly abundant, extremely long lifespans (trillions of years), easier to detect planets around them (deeper transits, larger radial velocity tugs).
    * *Cons:* High stellar activity (flares/X-rays) can strip atmospheres; close HZ leads to tidal locking (one side always faces the star).
* **Upper Bound on Earth-like planets around FGK stars:** Estimates vary widely based on the model, but generally range between 10% to 50% ($\eta_{\oplus}$). 
* **Period Constraints on Occurrence Rates:** Confirmed data is heavily biased toward short orbital periods because they transit more frequently and exert a stronger gravitational tug (easier to detect in a short observation window).
* **Hot Jupiters:** Gas giants on extremely short orbits (<10 days). They *appear* abundant in data because they are the easiest to detect, but true occurrence rate is low (~1%).
* **Occurrence Rates vs. Confirmed Data:** Confirmed planet counts show our *observational bias* (lots of Hot Jupiters). Occurrence rates correct for this bias to show the true statistical population (Mini-Neptunes and Super-Earths are actually the most common).
* **Exoplanet Classifications:** Hot Jupiters, Cold Jupiters, Neptunes, Mini-Neptunes, Super-Earths, and Earth-sized/Terrestrial.
* **Mass-Radius Relationship:** Helps determine bulk density. Rocky planets show radius increasing slowly with mass. Gas/ice-rich planets show a rapid radius increase for a small mass increase.
* **Bulk Densities:** Mass divided by Volume. High density implies a rocky/iron composition; low density implies a gas envelope or water-rich world.
* **Radius Valley (Fulton Gap):** A scarcity of planets between 1.5 and 2.0 Earth radii. It implies an evolutionary divide where planets just above this size hold onto volatile gas envelopes (Mini-Neptunes), while those below have had their atmospheres stripped away by stellar radiation (Super-Earths).
* **Missing from Solar System:** Super-Earths / Mini-Neptunes (sizes between Earth and Neptune). Hot Jupiters are also missing.
* **Eccentricity Distribution:** Exoplanets often have highly eccentric (elliptical) orbits, whereas Solar System planets have nearly circular orbits.
* **Close-in System Formation:** Formed further out beyond the ice line and migrated inward via disk migration or high-eccentricity migration (gravitational scattering + tidal circularization).
* **Biosignature Challenges:** Exoplanet atmospheres are incredibly thin/dim, clouds/hazes block spectra, and "abiotic false positives" exist (e.g., oxygen produced by UV light splitting water, not by life).
* **Reconciling Short Periods & Ice Line:** Planet migration. Gas giants and Mini-Neptunes need ice to form their cores, so they form past the snow line and migrate inward over time.

## Rocketry

* **The Rocket Equation (Tsiolkovsky):** $\Delta v = v_e \ln(m_0/m_f)$. It determines how much change in velocity ($\Delta v$) a rocket gets based on its exhaust velocity and the ratio of its initial mass (with fuel) to final mass (empty).
* **Principles:** Based on Newton's Third Law of Motion (action and equal/opposite reaction) and Conservation of Momentum. Rockets expel mass backward to push themselves forward—they do *not* need air to push against.
* **First Liquid-Fuelled Rocket:** Launched by Robert Goddard (1926).
* **Key Figures:**
    * **Wernher von Braun:** German aerospace engineer, developed V-2 rocket for Nazis, later led US Saturn V moon rocket development.
    * **William Moore:** British mathematician, early treatise on rocket motion and ballistics (1813).
    * **Konstantin Tsiolkovsky:** Russian pioneer of astronautic theory; derived the rocket equation.
    * **Robert Goddard:** American engineer; built/flew first liquid-fueled rocket.
    * **Hermann Oberth:** German/Romanian physicist; founding father of rocketry and astronautics, mentored von Braun.

## Space Exploration (Robotics)

* **First Rocket in Space:** The German V-2 rocket (1944) crossed the Kármán line. 
* **First Image of Earth from Space:** Taken by a camera on a captured V-2 rocket launched by the US in 1946.
* **Yuri Kondratyuk:** Ukrainian/Soviet engineer who developed the concept of Lunar Orbit Rendezvous (LOR), the strategy later used by the Apollo program.
* **Soviet Space Program Lead:** Sergei Korolev (the "Chief Designer").
* **ICBM Connections:** Early orbital rockets (like the Soviet R-7 and US Atlas) were originally designed as Intercontinental Ballistic Missiles to carry nuclear warheads.
* **Sputnik 1:** Launched Oct 4, 1957, by an R-7 Semyorka ICBM.
* **Luna Program Firsts:** Luna 1 (first to leave Earth's gravity/fly by Moon), Luna 2 (first human-made object to impact the Moon), Luna 3 (first photos of lunar far side), Luna 9 (first soft landing).
* **Mariner Probes:** * Mariner 2: First successful planetary flyby (Venus).
    * Mariner 4: First successful Mars flyby + first close-up photos of another planet.
    * Mariner 9: First spacecraft to orbit another planet (Mars).
    * Mariner 10: First to use gravity assist, first to visit Mercury.
* **First Mars Landings:** Soviet Mars 3 (1971, first soft landing but failed seconds later). US Viking 1 & 2 (1976, first fully successful landings and operations).
* **Venera & Vega Programs:** Soviet Venus missions. Venera achieved the first soft landing on Venus, first images from the surface, and first audio recording. Vega dropped balloons into Venus's atmosphere and flew by Halley's Comet.
* **Comet Missions:** Giotto (Halley's Comet flyby), Stardust (Wild 2 sample return), Rosetta/Philae (Orbited and landed on Churyumov–Gerasimenko).
* **Pioneer 10 & 11:** First to cross the asteroid belt, first to fly by Jupiter (and Saturn for 11), first to achieve escape velocity from the Solar System.
* **Pioneer Plaques:** Gold-anodized aluminum plaques with pictorial messages for extraterrestrials (human figures, pulsar map, hydrogen atom). Issues: Criticized as overly anthropocentric, highly cryptic to decode, and controversial for showing nudity.
* **Voyager Missions (1 & 2):** Farthest human-made objects. Conducted a "Grand Tour" of the outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). Voyager 1 & 2 have entered interstellar space.
* **The Golden Record:** A phonograph record on the Voyagers containing sounds, music, and greetings in 55 languages. *Difference from Pioneer:* Contains vastly more complex audio/visual data but requires building a record player to decode.
* **Cassini-Huygens:** NASA/ESA mission. Cassini orbited Saturn; the Huygens probe landed on Saturn's moon Titan.
* **Pluto Flyby:** New Horizons (2015).
* **First US Landing on "That Type of Body":** The NEAR Shoemaker probe made the first landing on an *asteroid* (Eros) in 2001.

## Space Exploration (Life and Crewed)

* **First Animals Launched:** Fruit flies launched by the US on a V-2 rocket (1947). They survived.
* **Early Space Flight Causes of Death:** Extreme heat stress, suffocation, equipment failure, or parachute/capsule recovery failures. 
* **First Primate to Survive:** Monkeys Miss Baker and Able (US suborbital flight, 1959). (Note: Yorick the monkey survived a 1951 flight but standard space lore often credits Baker/Able).
* **Laika:** Soviet street dog, first animal in orbit (Sputnik 2, 1957). Died of overheating/stress hours into the flight. It was a one-way mission; she was not meant to survive.
* **Dezik and Tsygan:** First dogs to make a suborbital flight and survive (Soviet, 1951).
* **Why Soviets Used Dogs:** They used stray dogs from Moscow streets, theorizing they were already accustomed to extreme cold, stress, and starvation.
* **Ham the Astrochimp:** Pushed levers in response to lights to prove motor control and cognitive function were possible in microgravity. Flight went higher and faster than planned, cabin lost pressure, but Ham survived.
* **First Nationals:**
    * *Soviet:* Yuri Gagarin (1961) - Orbital, first human in space.
    * *American:* Alan Shepard (1961) - Suborbital (John Glenn did orbital in 1962).
    * *Chinese:* Yang Liwei (2003) - Orbital.
* **First Women:**
    * *USSR:* Valentina Tereshkova (1963).
    * *US:* Sally Ride (1983).
    * *China:* Liu Yang (2012).
* **Tereshkova's Distinction:** She remains the only woman to have flown a *solo* space mission. She was also a civilian (textile worker).
* **Alan Shepard's Unmet Need:** Bathroom facilities. The flight was severely delayed on the pad, and he had to urinate in his spacesuit.
* **Wally Funk:** Member of the "Mercury 13" (women who passed astronaut testing but were denied by NASA). She finally flew to space in 2021 on Blue Origin at age 82.
* **Zond 5:** Soviet circumlunar mission (1968). Carried tortoises, wine flies, mealworms, and plants. First Earth life to travel to and orbit the Moon and return safely.
* **Komarov's Death:** Soyuz 1 (1967). Parachutes failed on reentry; crashed into the ground. (Note: The *only* people to die completely in the vacuum of *outer space* were the Soviet Soyuz 11 crew in 1971 due to depressurization).
* **First Multi-Crewed Missions:** Voskhod 1 (Soviet, 1964) and Gemini 3 (US, 1965).
* **First Crewed Moon Landing:** Apollo 11 (July 20, 1969). Crew: Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin, Michael Collins.
* **Katherine Johnson:** NASA mathematician and "human computer" whose orbital mechanics calculations were critical to the success of early US crewed spaceflights (Shepard, Glenn, Apollo).
* **ESA 2007 Tardigrade Experiment:** FOTON-M3 mission exposed tardigrades (water bears) directly to the vacuum of space and solar radiation. Many survived and successfully reproduced after returning.

## Space Stations

* **First Space Stations:** Soviet Union: Salyut 1 (1971). US: Skylab (1973).
* **Leader in Long-Term Habitation:** The Soviet Union (via the Salyut and Mir programs).
* **Peaceful Only?** No. Early Soviet space stations included the highly classified *Almaz* military stations, which were equipped with spy cameras and an actual rapid-fire cannon.
* **NASA Littering Fine:** Skylab's orbit decayed in 1979, raining debris over Esperance, Western Australia. The local council jokingly fined NASA $400 for littering.
* **Apollo-Soyuz Test Project (1975):** First joint US-Soviet spaceflight. Eased Cold War tensions and proved rival nations could collaborate in space.
* **Androgynous Peripheral Attach System (APAS):** Necessary so any two spacecraft could dock. Eliminated the need for one ship to be "male" and the other "female," allowing any ship to act as a rescue vessel.
* **Mir Significance:** First modular space station; held the record for longest continuous human presence for a long time. *Geopolitical Event:* The Soviet Union collapsed (1991) while cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev was on board (he went up Soviet and came down Russian).
* **Early Post-Apollo-Soyuz Cooperation:** The Shuttle-Mir program (1990s). US Space Shuttles docked with the Russian Mir space station.
* **ISS Non-Science Mission:** Space diplomacy. It forces deep geopolitical cooperation, keeping the US and Russia collaborating even during extreme terrestrial conflicts.
* **ISS Astrobiology Uses:** Exposure facilities (like EXPOSE) bolt microbes/organics to the outside of the station to test survival in space vacuum/radiation. Also used to test plant growth in microgravity (Veggie).
* **Survivability & Reproduction in Space:** Microbes, fungi, and tardigrades can survive extreme space conditions. Reproduction is successful for simpler organisms (plants, insects, nematodes) but highly problematic for mammals due to radiation and microgravity's effects on gestation.
* **Tiangong:** Chinese space station (completed ~2022). Roughly 20% the mass of the ISS, but operates similarly as a permanently crewed, modular orbital laboratory.
* **ISS Proposed End-of-Life:** ~2030 (planned to be safely deorbited into the South Pacific Ocean).
* **Moon Trees:** Seeds taken into lunar orbit by Apollo 14 astronaut Stuart Roosa, then brought back and planted globally to see if spaceflight affected their growth.
* **Grown on the Far Side of the Moon:** Cotton seeds sprouted inside a sealed biosphere canister on China's Chang'e 4 lander in 2019 (died shortly after when the lunar night froze them).


# Exoplanet Detection & Math Cheat Sheet

## 1. Core Formulas & Math

**Constants to Know:**
* **G (Gravitational Constant):** 6.674 x 10^-11 kg^-1 m^3 s^-2
* **c (Speed of Light):** 3.0 x 10^8 m/s
* **1 AU:** 1.496 x 10^11 m
* **M_sun (Solar Mass):** 1.989 x 10^30 kg
* **R_sun (Solar Radius):** 696,340 km
* **M_jup (Jupiter Mass):** 1.898 x 10^27 kg
* **R_jup (Jupiter Radius):** 69,911 km
* *Note: Always convert days/years to seconds, and km to meters for gravity calculations!*

**Transit Depth (Percent Drop in Flux):**
* `Depth (%) = (R_planet / R_star)^2 * 100`
* *Use this to find the planet's radius. Make sure both radii are in the same units before dividing.*

**Doppler Shift (Radial Velocity):**
* `v = c * (Δλ / λ_0)`
* `v` = Radial velocity (m/s)
* `Δλ` = Wavelength shift
* `λ_0` = Rest wavelength

**Radial Velocity Amplitude (K):**
* Assuming a circular, edge-on orbit (sin *i* = 1) and that the planet's mass is much smaller than the star's (m_p << M_star):
* `K = [ m_planet / (M_star)^(2/3) ] * [ (2 * π * G) / P ]^(1/3)`
* `P` = Orbital period (in seconds)
* `m_planet` and `M_star` must be in kg. K will be in m/s.

**Blackbody Flux Ratio (Direct Imaging):**
* Used to find the contrast between the star and planet at a specific wavelength (λ):
* `F_star / F_planet = (R_star / R_planet)^2 * [ (e^(hc / λkT_planet) - 1) / (e^(hc / λkT_star) - 1) ]`
* *Shortcut for λ = 10 μm:* The exponent term `(hc / λk)` evaluates to approximately `1438.8 K`. So you can use `e^(1438.8 / T)` for the intensity factors.

## 2. Detection Methods Summary

**Transit Method**
* **What it measures:** The dip in a star's brightness when a planet passes in front of it.
* **What it tells us:** Planet's radius, orbital period, orbital distance, and inclination (must be edge-on, ~90 degrees).
* **Bias:** Heavily favors large planets on short orbital periods (close to the star).

**Radial Velocity (Doppler) Method**
* **What it measures:** The blue-shift and red-shift of a star's spectral lines as it wobbles due to the planet's gravity.
* **What it tells us:** The planet's *minimum* mass (M sin *i*) and orbital period.
* **Bias:** Favors massive planets on short orbital periods.

**Transit + Radial Velocity Synergy**
* If a planet transits, we know its orbit is edge-on (sin *i* = 1).
* This allows us to turn the RV "minimum mass" into **true mass**.
* Mass (from RV) + Volume (from Transit Radius) = **Bulk Density** (tells us if it's rocky, gas, ice, etc.).

**Direct Imaging**
* **What it measures:** Actual photons (light) from the planet.
* **Best conditions:** Works best in the **infrared** where young, hot giant planets glow brightly, and the host star is relatively dimmer (better contrast ratio).
* **Bias:** Favors massive, young planets on *very wide* orbits (so they aren't lost in the star's glare).

**Astrometry**
* **What it measures:** The physical 2D movement (wobble) of a star across the plane of the sky relative to distant background stars.
* **Best conditions:** Easier to detect around **nearby stars** (larger angular shift). Works well for massive planets on wide orbits.

**Gravitational Microlensing**
* **What it measures:** The temporary magnification of a distant background star when a foreground star (and its planet) pass in front of it, bending the light.
* **Strengths:** Can detect planets far from their host stars (at or beyond the "snow line") and doesn't require seeing the planet's actual light.

**Pulsar Timing**
* **What it measures:** Variations in the incredibly precise, stable arrival times of radio pulses from a pulsar.
* **Strengths:** Capable of detecting remarkably small (Earth-mass or smaller) planets.
